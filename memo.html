The Clipboard Problem

White Paper | May 2026

← Back to Truss

Executive Summary

North American banks extend more than $289 billion in asset-based credit lines secured by physical collateral: inventory, equipment fleets, and vehicle floors that lenders can neither see in real time nor verify continuously. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency mandates field audits as a cornerstone of sound ABL risk management, yet the method of conducting those audits has not materially changed in four decades: a human examiner, a clipboard, and a camera roll. The examiner pool is contracting. Fraud schemes that exploit collateral opacity are growing more sophisticated.

We propose a three-component solution: a structured mobile capture application that guides examiners through a standardized photographic protocol; a vision-language model (VLM) inference pipeline that reads serial plates, scores asset condition against reference libraries, and flags discrepancies; and a bank-formatted audit report delivered within 48 hours rather than two weeks. The result is a machine-verifiable audit trail, an estimated 85% reduction in per-asset examiner time, and a compliance record that regulators can query programmatically.

The switching cost is the data. Each completed audit enriches a proprietary reference library that widens the moat, improves model accuracy, and makes the platform progressively more defensible. This paper presents the market structure, regulatory context, fraud landscape, technical architecture, and business model supporting this thesis.

1. Market Structure

Asset-based lending is a foundational instrument of commercial credit. Rather than extending credit against a borrower's cash flows or EBITDA multiples, ABL structures a revolving facility around the liquidation value of pledged assets — most commonly accounts receivable, inventory, and equipment. The borrowing base expands and contracts as those assets are counted, valued, and verified.

The market is large and growing. The global ABL market was valued at approximately $690 billion in 2023 and is projected to approach $2.1 trillion by 2033, compounding at roughly 12% annually. North America dominates, holding over 42% of global volume — approximately $290 billion in committed revolving credit as of 2023, with U.S. ABL commitments alone reaching $502 billion by end-2022 according to the Secured Finance Network. Inventory financing and equipment financing together represent the plurality of that collateral base, and it is precisely these asset classes where physical verification is most operationally demanding and most susceptible to fraud.

The economics of ABL create a natural verification problem. A borrower draws against a self-reported schedule of eligible collateral. The lender advances funds within defined advance rates (typically 50–85% of eligible inventory value, higher for receivables). Between field audits, the lender has no independent view of whether the physical assets on the certificate actually exist at the represented location, condition, or quantity. The audit is the only ground truth.

2. The Regulatory Architecture

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency has published extensive guidance on ABL risk management in its Comptroller's Handbook. The Handbook is unambiguous: "Field audits are integral to monitoring and controlling ABL. A field audit helps detect fraud and financial weakness and is a customary way to confirm the quality of the borrower's financial data, receivables, inventory, and internal controls." The OCC further specifies that field audit standards (including frequency, scope, and documentation requirements) must be formally established in each bank's written ABL policies, subject to initial and periodic board-level review and approval.

The OCC's guidance identifies field audits as one of the primary mechanisms for managing what it describes as ABL's most critical exposure: credit risk amplified by "characteristics of higher default risk, such as high leverage, erratic cash flows, limited working capital, and frequently changing collateral pools." For high-risk or complex facilities, quarterly on-site verification has been the industry standard.

The methodology prescribed by this framework has not changed materially since it was codified. A field examiner visits the borrower's premises, physically counts or spot-checks inventory, photographs assets and serial plates, compares observations against the borrowing base certificate, and writes a narrative report. The OCC's verification procedures for accounts receivable and inventory financing describe this as a labor-intensive process: examiners must verify borrower compliance with the loan agreement, identify trends in inventory quality and turnover, check for obsolete or ineligible stock, and reconcile physical counts against reported collateral pools.

Notably, as of January 2026, the OCC has moved to reduce mandatory policy-based examination requirements for community banks, instead empowering examiners to tailor scope based on risk. This shift toward risk-based supervision increases the premium on high-quality, data-rich collateral monitoring — precisely what a machine-assisted audit trail provides.

3. The Fraud Problem

ABL is, in the words of the OCC Comptroller's Handbook, "particularly susceptible to borrower fraud, especially when a business experiences financial difficulties." This observation is not rhetorical. Expert practitioners confirm the pattern: "Most lenders and workout specialists agree that, when compared to other forms of secured lending, asset-based lending is particularly susceptible to fraud — especially when borrowers experience unpredictable cash flow and financial troubles." When businesses face liquidity pressure, the temptation to borrow against inflated or nonexistent collateral is acute.

The mechanics of inventory fraud are well-documented. Perpetrators may create two sets of books, generate false purchase orders and invoices, or — in the most brazen cases — fill warehouses with empty boxes purportedly containing inventory. Receivables-side fraud involves "fresh air invoicing," the creation of fictitious sales to inflate the borrowing base. The manual spot-check methodology that governs most field audits is poorly designed to detect either variety: an examiner counting boxes cannot weigh them, and an examiner photographing a label cannot verify the provenance of the invoice that underlies it.

The broader fraud environment in which ABL operates is deteriorating. Nasdaq's 2024 Global Financial Crime Report estimated $485.6 billion in global fraud and scam losses in 2023, with the Americas accounting for $151.1 billion. The LexisNexis True Cost of Fraud study found that every dollar lost to fraud costs North American financial institutions $4.41 when fees, investigation costs, and remediation are included. In auto lending alone — a close analogue to vehicle floor plan ABL — Point Predictive's 2025 report identified $9.2 billion in fraud risk exposure from a single year of analyzed applications.

The convergence of a growing collateral base, a static verification methodology, and an increasingly sophisticated fraud environment is the structural gap this platform addresses.

4. The Labor Problem

Field examination is a specialized discipline. ABL field examiners combine accounting knowledge, inventory expertise, industry-specific collateral familiarity, and physical investigative skill. This workforce is aging. The broader finance and accounting sector has lost approximately 340,000 (17%) practitioners over the past five years, driven by retirements and career transitions. Recruiting difficulty for audit and examination roles hit its highest recorded level in early 2024 according to industry tracking surveys. Simultaneously, 76% of U.S. CFOs report significant talent shortages in their finance and accounting functions.

For ABL specifically, the examiner shortage compounds the verification problem. A single examiner assigned to a large industrial borrower with several hundred pieces of equipment may spend days on a single audit — days that grow harder to staff as the experienced cohort retires and replacements are not forthcoming. Banks with smaller ABL portfolios are particularly exposed, as they often cannot justify a dedicated field examination team and rely on outsourced examiners whose availability and quality are increasingly constrained.

The implication is a widening gap between the collateral monitoring that regulations require and the human capacity available to deliver it. Technology is the only path to closing that gap without accepting either reduced audit coverage or material increases in compliance cost.

5. The Platform: Three Components, One Audit Trail

5.1 Guided Mobile Capture

The examiner's workflow begins with a structured mobile application that imposes a consistent photographic and data-entry protocol across every audit engagement. Rather than providing the examiner with open-ended discretion — which introduces variability, creates documentation gaps, and produces results that are difficult to compare across time or across borrowers — the application sequences the examiner through asset-by-asset capture tasks.

For each asset on the collateral schedule, the application presents a capture checklist: serial plate photograph from a defined angle, overall condition photograph, context photograph showing warehouse or yard position, and structured data entry for any anomalies observed. The application enforces completeness before allowing the examiner to advance to the next asset. GPS coordinates and timestamps are embedded in each capture. The result is a structured dataset rather than an unordered camera roll and a handwritten checklist.

5.2 VLM Inference Pipeline

Captured images enter a cloud-based inference pipeline anchored by a fine-tuned vision-language model. The pipeline performs three primary functions.

Serial plate extraction. The VLM reads manufacturer serial numbers, VINs, equipment identification numbers, and asset tags from photographs. Recent research demonstrates that fine-tuned VLMs achieve strong performance on plate and serial recognition even under challenging field conditions — partial occlusion, low light, weather degradation, and closely spaced characters. A domain-adapted model built on architectures such as PaliGemma, with LoRA fine-tuning, has demonstrated plate recognition accuracy of 87–92% on challenging real-world datasets, representing significant improvements over legacy OCR pipelines that compound errors across multi-stage architectures.

Extracted serial numbers are matched against the collateral schedule and, where data is available, against manufacturer databases and title registries. Discrepancies — an asset on the schedule that cannot be found, a serial number that maps to a different asset class, a VIN that appears on multiple facilities — are flagged for examiner review.

Condition scoring. The VLM scores asset condition on a structured rubric against reference images from manufacturer specifications and historical audit records of comparable assets. Scores are calibrated to the asset class: condition scoring for a fleet of over-the-road trailers requires different reference libraries than scoring for CNC machine tools or dealer vehicle floor plans. Condition scores inform eligible collateral calculations and, over time, generate depreciation curves that improve the accuracy of advance rate recommendations.

Anomaly detection. Beyond individual asset assessment, the pipeline analyzes the population of captured assets for structural anomalies: assets photographed in an unexpected location, assets that appear in the same position in every audit period without movement consistent with active inventory turnover, concentrations of assets with similar condition scores that are statistically inconsistent with reported usage patterns. These signals are weighted and aggregated into a facility-level risk score that supplements the per-asset findings.

5.3 Bank-Formatted Audit Report

Pipeline outputs are assembled into a structured audit report formatted to match the bank's existing credit administration templates. The report includes: a collateral schedule reconciliation showing matched, unmatched, and anomalous assets; per-asset condition scores with supporting images; a facility-level risk narrative; and an audit trail that logs every capture event with timestamp, GPS coordinate, and model version. Delivery target is 48 hours from completion of field capture.

The audit trail is machine-verifiable. Every finding is traceable to a specific image, a specific model inference, and a specific capture event. Examiners can annotate findings, and their annotations are preserved alongside model outputs. Regulatory examiners reviewing a bank's ABL compliance can query the audit database programmatically rather than reviewing physical binders.

6. Efficiency Economics

The time-reduction claim of 85% per-asset examiner time warrants unpacking. Under the current methodology, examiner time is consumed by: travel between assets within a facility, manual serial number transcription and cross-referencing, condition narrative writing, photography without a structured protocol (leading to re-shoots and documentation gaps), and report compilation and formatting. For a facility with 200 assets, each step multiplies.

The platform eliminates manual transcription (the VLM reads serial plates), standardizes photography (the guided protocol eliminates re-shoots and documentation gaps), and automates report compilation (the structured pipeline replaces hours of formatted narrative writing). The examiner's role shifts from data entry and report writing to anomaly investigation and professional judgment on flagged items. This is the same labor reallocation that has driven efficiency gains in AI-assisted document review, where firms deploying AI in due diligence have achieved up to 30% cost reductions with improvement in decision speed and accuracy — and where the gains concentrate precisely in the highest-volume, most repetitive processing tasks.

The 48-hour report delivery compresses the feedback loop from weeks to days. Under the current methodology, a field examination report may not reach the credit officer until weeks after the physical visit, by which time the collateral pool may have changed materially. Faster delivery reduces the window during which undetected collateral deterioration or fraud can compound.

7. The Data Moat

The most durable competitive advantage this platform generates is not the software or the inference pipeline. It is the proprietary reference library that accumulates with each completed audit.

Every audit adds to a dataset of: asset condition photographs linked to asset class, age, utilization, and industry; serial plate images that train and validate the extraction model; condition scoring outcomes that are confirmed or corrected by examiner annotations; and fraud signals that are retrospectively validated when facilities enter workout. No competitor who enters the market in year three has access to two years of labeled, validated, industry-specific collateral imagery. This is the structural switching cost embedded in the product.

Banks that adopt the platform first accumulate a richer longitudinal record of their collateral portfolios than any competitor. The audit trail becomes a credit risk asset in its own right — a queryable history of collateral condition that informs advance rate decisions, early warning indicators, and workout recovery estimates in a way that no clipboard-based system can produce.

8. Regulatory Alignment and Risk

The platform does not replace the field examiner. It augments the examiner's effectiveness and produces a richer, more defensible audit record. This framing is intentional and correct. The OCC's guidance emphasizes that field audits require professional judgment — the assessment of borrower cooperation, the evaluation of inventory management practices, the identification of management red flags that are not captured in a photograph. The platform handles the mechanical work; the examiner provides the judgment.

This architecture is well-aligned with the direction of OCC supervision. The OCC's 2025 shift toward risk-based examination for community banks explicitly encourages reliance on technology-enhanced monitoring systems and bank-provided reports that can be validated for reliability. A machine-verifiable audit trail produced by a standardized capture-and-inference workflow is precisely the kind of instrument that supports this model.

The principal regulatory risk is model reliability. If the VLM produces serial plate extractions or condition scores that are systematically biased — underestimating deterioration in a specific asset class, for instance — the consequences could be material. Mitigation requires: ongoing model validation against examiner-confirmed ground truth; clear disclosure to banks of model limitations and confidence intervals; mandatory examiner review of flagged items; and a formal human-in-the-loop protocol that preserves examiner authority over final collateral determinations.

A secondary risk is data security. Collateral photographs, serial numbers, and facility locations constitute sensitive commercial information. The platform must operate under a security architecture appropriate for regulated financial data — SOC 2 Type II certification at minimum, with data residency options for banks with specific compliance requirements.

9. Go-to-Market Strategy

The natural entry point is mid-market ABL lenders: institutions with $500 million to $5 billion in committed ABL exposure who lack the scale to build proprietary field examination technology but face the same regulatory requirements and fraud risks as the largest banks. This segment is large enough to generate meaningful revenue and transaction volume, operationally sophisticated enough to understand the value proposition, and underserved by existing vendors whose solutions are oriented toward financial data ingestion rather than physical collateral verification.

The initial wedge is vehicle floor plan — dealer inventory financing secured by individual automobiles, trucks, and recreational vehicles. Floor plan is the highest-volume, most standardized physical collateral class in ABL. Every asset has a VIN. Condition scoring criteria are well-established. Reference libraries are available from manufacturer specifications. The audit workflow is highly repetitive and therefore highly automatable. A floor plan-focused deployment establishes proof of concept, generates labeled training data at scale, and builds the reference library foundation for expansion into heavier equipment classes.

Expansion follows a collateral-class roadmap: vehicle floor plan → construction equipment → manufacturing equipment → mixed industrial inventory. Each class requires a purpose-built reference library and fine-tuning of the condition scoring model, but the core capture, inference, and reporting infrastructure is shared.

Pricing is structured as a per-audit transaction fee plus a platform subscription for data access and reporting infrastructure. The transaction fee is calibrated against the cost of a traditional field examination engagement — typically several thousand dollars per day of examiner time for outsourced providers — creating a value proposition that is compelling even before efficiency gains are factored in.

10. Closing

The asset-based lending market is large, growing, and structurally dependent on physical collateral verification that has not materially modernized in four decades. The regulatory framework mandates rigorous field audits. The examiner workforce is contracting. Fraud losses in the broader lending ecosystem are mounting. The economics of AI-assisted verification — faster delivery, lower per-asset cost, machine-verifiable audit trails — are compelling and increasingly achievable as vision-language models demonstrate robust performance on precisely the recognition tasks that field examination requires.

The platform described here is not a replacement for the experienced field examiner. It is an instrument that extends the examiner's reach, standardizes their documentation, and transforms the output of their visit into a queryable, defensible, continuously improving record. The data accumulated across audits becomes the asset — a proprietary collateral intelligence layer that no legacy provider can replicate and no new entrant can quickly match.

The clipboard had a good run. Its time is over.
